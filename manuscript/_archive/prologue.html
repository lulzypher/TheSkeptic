The Case for a Case File

Every investigation begins with something that was seen. A witness statement. A mark on the pavement. A number that does not fit the pattern. What separates a real investigation from an argument is what happens next: the investigator refuses to let the first impression stand unexamined, and instead assembles a record in which every link is labeled — what was observed, what was inferred, and what remains unknown.

This book is written in that style. It concerns a question that is usually asked casually, and here I want to ask it carefully: what do we actually know, about the world outside our minds and about the machinery of the world itself, and how well do we know it? Not "do we know anything?" — skepticism in that form is not a position but a mood. The question worth answering is graded: on each claim we hold, how much evidence stands behind it, what assumptions are doing load-bearing work, and what would have to be true for the claim to be wrong?

Why the case-file format? Because most disputes about science are not really disputes about the findings. They are disputes about what the findings establish. "The experiment was performed" and "the conclusion follows" are two different statements, and the gap between them is where the whole book lives. In each chapter I will therefore close with an evidence ledger: a table with three columns — Observed, Inferred, and Unknown. Observed is what the instruments or witnesses actually delivered. Inferred is what the interpretation added. Unknown is what the claim still requires that has not yet been shown.

I will use the ledger on big claims — quarks, the Standard Model, general relativity — and on smaller, more human ones — whether a witness's memory is reliable, whether a dream reports anything real. The format forces the same discipline onto both, which is the point: skepticism should scale. A claim about the Higgs boson and a claim about what a neighbor saw on a dark street should each be held to the standard that its specific evidence can support, no more, no less.

One more convention before the first file opens. When I say "we know" I mean "the scientific community has built a body of mutually supporting measurements, and no available alternative predicts the observations as well." When I say "we do not know" I mean "there is a live question, and I will tell you what the live question is and what would answer it." You will see that the boundary is far more productive than either a shrug or a shrill correction. The honest map of knowledge is neither the blank sheet the skeptic prefers nor the finished atlas the enthusiast prefers. It is a chart with surveyed regions, and coastlines that are themselves survey findings — real, useful, and revisable.
