Prologue · How We Decide What We Know

The Bone That Shouldn't Last?

In 2010, a researcher in Montana cut open a piece of Tyrannosaurus rex bone that had been sitting in a drawer for about a decade. Inside, instead of rock where the living tissue should have been, she saw thin, branching, translucent structures — they looked, under the microscope, like the remains of blood vessels. She had studied this bone for years. She had never seen them before. And the reason she told the journal that published her finding was simple: she didn't know what to do with them. If the bone is sixty-six million years old, that tissue should not be there. If the tissue is real, something the field believed about how long decay takes — the field's central working assumption — is wrong.

This is the book's opening case, and it was chosen not because the answer is settled, but because the structure of the question is exactly what this book is about. The question is not "is the tissue dinosaur, or is it contamination?" The question is: given a surprising observation, an established framework, and a model sitting in between, what do you examine first? The observation? The framework? The model? Or the assumptions connecting all three? Everything in Part I is built to answer that question, and this prologue is where you watch it happen.

0.1 · The observation, as reported What was actually claimed

To be precise about what is being disputed, the claims need to be separated, because the popular versions blur them together. Three distinct lines of evidence have been reported, by independent laboratories, over roughly a decade.

First, the structural reports. In 2010, Horner, Padian, and others described translucent, branching structures inside a T. rex femur, which they called "possibly preserved soft tissue." In 2012, a larger team — Hone, Norell, and colleagues — published a review in Biological Reviews that collected the same kind of observation across multiple specimens: vessel-like and fibre-like structures inside Late Cretaceous bone. These are structural claims. They are about what the microscope shows, not about what the material is chemically.

Second, the fibril reports. In 2007 and again in 2010, Schweitzer's group reported collagen fibrils in T. rex bone — thin filaments with a characteristic banding pattern, called the D-period, that is a signature of collagen. In 2010 they reported keratin-like filaments as well. These are biochemical claims. They are about the molecular structure of material inside the bone.

Third, the sequence reports. In 2015, the same group published proteomic data: they identified protein fragments in the T. rex bone that matched collagen sequences. This is the strongest claim, and the one that drew the most criticism, because sequence matching depends on databases, on search algorithms, and on the assumption that the protein has not degraded enough to change its sequence beyond recognition.

0.2 · The framework, as believed What the field assumed

Now the assumption being tested. Before 2005, the working model in paleobiology was this: soft tissue degrades completely within a few thousand years under most burial conditions. Bone survives because it is dense, mineralised, and chemically stable. But the collagen and proteins inside it — the organic fraction — break down at the molecular level, lose their sequence, lose their structure, and eventually become indistinguishable from the surrounding minerals. Sixty-six million years is about one thousand times longer than even the most generous versions of that model allow.

So when the structures appeared under the microscope, the field's first instinct was to treat them as contamination. That instinct deserves respect, but it is worth being precise about what the respect is for. It is not that the framework it serves is right — established frameworks blind as easily as they clarify, and "survived decades of successful prediction" is not the same as "true." It is that overturning one of them is a heavy claim, and the honest response to a heavy claim is caution, not conviction. When an observation contradicts the established model, the observation can be wrong, or it can be contaminated, or it can be misread — so the weight of prior evidence falls on the framework. That weight does not settle the question. What it does is set the standard the observation has to clear, and that standard is high because the stakes are real. In many fields, a claim that breaks the established system costs money, threatens careers, and puts its authors on the wrong side of their own institutions. The people whose capital — grants, positions, patents, industries — is built on the old answer will defend it, sometimes with papers written against your discovery, and in some places, against some people, with more than papers. So the rule is not "trust the framework." It is this: before you announce that your finding breaks the system, make sure the finding is right enough to survive the full force of the people whose answer it replaces. The first move, then, is still to treat the observation as the thing that has to be proven, not the thing that gets to be believed — and that applies to the "contamination" answer just as much as to anything else.

But "contamination" is a hypothesis, not a conclusion. It is a claim that modern DNA or modern protein got into the bone from some outside source — from the handling, from the lab, from the groundwater, from the researchers themselves. And a hypothesis needs evidence. It needs a mechanism by which modern collagen could have gotten into the bone and be preserved in the same structural form as the original tissue. And it needs to explain why the contamination would look exactly like what a blood vessel would look like, in the same positions, with the same D-period banding pattern.

This is the first lesson of the prologue, and it is the one that will repeat in every case in this book: a default assumption is not evidence. The field had a default — "that's contamination" — and the default was reasonable. But it was still an assumption, and assumptions, no matter how reasonable, do not explain data. They are what you fall back on when you have no explanation. The moment a competing explanation becomes available, the default has to earn its place by actually fitting the evidence better than the alternative.

0.3 · The method, applied What to examine first

So what do you do? You do not ask "is it dinosaur, or is it contamination?" You ask what would have to be true for each side to be right, and then you check whether those conditions hold.

If the tissue is genuine dinosaur collagen, then: (a) it should be chemically distinct from modern collagen in predictable ways — old proteins accumulate specific chemical modifications, like cross-links and deamidation, that are time-dependent; (b) it should be in the right positions, matching the internal structure of the bone; (c) it should be present in multiple specimens, in multiple labs, with the same structural features; (d) the bone should show evidence that the collagen was there before mineralisation completed, or that the mineral matrix preserved it in place.

If the tissue is modern contamination, then: (a) it should be chemically identical to modern collagen, without the time-dependent modifications; (b) it should be in positions that are consistent with an intrusion pathway — cracks, surface deposits, lab reagent traces; (c) it should be easier to reproduce or eliminate with better handling; (d) the same structures should not appear in the same positions across independent specimens.

The method, then, is not "check the observation." The observation is what it is. The method is: identify the assumptions that connect the observation to the conclusion, and check each one. The connection between "I see vessel-like structures" and "this is dinosaur blood vessel tissue" is a chain of inferences, and each link in that chain is a place where error can enter. The chain is: the structure is not an artifact of the microscope → the material is not modern contamination → the material is not a mineral mimic → the material is collagen → the collagen is dinosaur → the collagen has not degraded beyond recognition.

Every one of those links is a separate claim, and every one of them is separately checkable. And the method does not require you to resolve all of them before forming a provisional view. It requires you to know which ones you have checked, which ones you have not, and which ones would change your view if they failed. That is the difference between having an opinion and having a position.

That difference is what this book is about. The next chapters will walk through each link in the chain, and you will see the method applied to each one. But first, the full ledger — every claim, every assumption, every status — in one table.

Nothing in that table is settled. That is not a defect. A case where every link is verified is not a case — it is a textbook entry. The work begins at the first unresolved row, and the method continues row by row. The prologue is the method in miniature: here is the observation, here is the framework it clashes with, here are the links in the chain, here is the first row that has to be checked. The next chapters check them, one at a time.

And the reason this book is called what it is: none of this requires you to decide in advance what you believe. You just have to be willing to say, for each row, which side of the ledger it is on. That is all the skepticism it is, and it is not very much. The rest is the rest of the book.

Exercise 1 — Find a default in your own work

Pick one claim you have defended recently — a meeting argument, a design decision, a conclusion in a report. Write it as a single sentence. Then answer the three questions the method asks:

  1. What is the observation? What evidence did you actually see, versus what you assumed was visible? (A test result you ran versus a result someone told you about.)
  2. What is the default framework? What prior model makes this observation surprising or impossible? What does "normal" look like in your field?
  3. What would change your mind? Write one concrete, testable condition — not "more evidence" — that would move you off the claim. If you cannot write it, the claim is not a claim; it is a position.

Keep the three sentences. You will use the same three questions on the fossil in every chapter that follows, and the format is the book's unit of progress. If you can do it on a claim that is easy and low-stakes, the case in this book becomes a matter of doing the same work with a higher-stakes claim.

Verdict

The observation is real. Multiple labs, multiple animals, multiple techniques. The structures and the material chemistry are documented, and the paper that reports them is not a fringe paper — it is a case with a name, a date, and a body of follow-up work. What is not documented, and what this book is about, is the chain of inference that connects that observation to "this is dinosaur protein that survived for 66 million years." Some links in that chain are solid. At least one is not. The rest of the book is a row-by-row walk through that ledger — which links hold, which do not, and where the work is actually happening right now.

The prologue's verdict, stated plainly: this is a real scientific case, not a conspiracy, and not a settled fact. Your job reading it is not to pick a side. Your job is to know which side of the ledger each claim sits on, and to be able to say, for the link you find weakest, what evidence would change that. If you can do that, you are ahead of most of the people arguing about this fossil on the internet.

Still open — whether any of the material is dinosaur, whether preservation mechanisms can explain the chemistry, and whether the two camps are arguing about the same question. Chapter 1 handles the first two. Chapter 2 handles the third.
