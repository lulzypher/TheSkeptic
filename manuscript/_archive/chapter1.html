Case file 1
Part I · Seeing

Seeing Is Not the Same as Knowing

When you look at a glass of water on a desk, you see the glass, the water, the desk, the light coming from one direction, the shadow falling from the other. You do not see the water molecules, and yet you believe there are water molecules in the glass. That gap — between what arrives in the eye and what you take to be true — is the entire subject of the book. This chapter examines it, because everything else in the project, from quarks to dreams, stands on whether that gap can be crossed honestly.

The claim I want to test is deceptively small: seeing is not the same as knowing, and the difference is not a failure of perception but a feature of the world. If I can defend that claim, then "I saw it myself" is not the end of an argument; it is the beginning of one. If I cannot defend it, then the skeptic's tool — the ledger of Observed / Inferred / Unknown — has no first row to write in, and the book needs a different shape.

That the unattended parts were "not there" or "irrelevant," when in fact they were merely unrepresented in this particular output. Whether the specific objects that captured your attention are the ones that mattered for the conclusion being drawn from this scene.
None of the above was written down. The description of the scene was formed in real time, in memory, and has already been edited. That the memory of the scene is a faithful record. In fact, the first "version" of the scene was already a reconstruction, not a recording. How much of what you remember of the scene is the scene, and how much is the mind's model of what the scene should have contained.
