# The Clock in the Rock

> **Chapter 5 — The Skeptic**
> *Part II · The World We Stand On*

---

A rock does not say "4.54 billion years." It says a ratio of two kinds of atom — one that started as a radioactive parent, one that is the daughter of its decay. The number "4.54 billion" is not read from the rock. It is *computed*, from three quantities the rock does not directly hand you, and every one of them rests on a separate measurement done elsewhere.

This is the oldest number in science. It is also, according to its critics, the most circular: the method uses constants calibrated against rocks of known age, and the known age of the rocks is established by the method. The critics are not wrong that the loop exists. They are wrong that the loop is closed — because the constants were not measured on rocks at all. They were measured, one by one, on the bench, in the laboratory, on quantities a rock never touches.

This chapter walks that loop step by step, and shows where it is anchored to the wall and where it is not.

## The claim on the table

The Earth is approximately 4.54 billion years old. The oldest known terrestrial zircons date to roughly 4.4 billion years. These are not inferences from a model. They are the output of a measurement chain that starts with a ratio of atoms and ends with a number.

The accusation, in its common form: radiometric dating is circular. The decay constants are determined by measuring rocks of known age, and the age is determined by the decay constants. If you accept one, you must accept the other, and the measurement has told you nothing.

The chapter's job: to separate the parts of that loop that are measured independently from the parts that are assumed, and to show the reader exactly where the ground ends and the inference begins.

## What the instrument actually returns

A mass spectrometer does not return a date. It returns a ratio.

In the uranium-lead system, the instrument measures the present abundance of uranium-238, uranium-235, and the lead isotopes they produce — lead-206, lead-207 — in a zircon grain no larger than a speck of dust. It gives you, for a single grain, something like: a parent-daughter ratio in one system, and a parent-daughter ratio in a second, independent system, in the same crystal, at the same time.

From those ratios, and from the decay constant of each isotope, a calculation follows:

* the ratio of daughter to parent gives a "model age" for each system
* if the two systems give the same age, the grain is **concordant** — it has been a closed system
* if they do not agree, the grain is **discordant** — it has lost or gained uranium or lead at some point, and the age is no longer a single number

Concordance is the key concept. A single concordant grain is a strong result. A population of grains plotting along a straight line — a **concordia** — is a much stronger result, because the intercept of that line on the upper end gives the age of crystallization, and the lower intercept gives the age of the disturbance, and the two are constrained by the same data. The calculation is not a single number from a single measurement. It is a geometric constraint on a set of measurements.

## The three assumptions

Every radiometric date rests on exactly three assumptions. Name them. Test them. Do not skip them.

**1. The system was closed.** No uranium was added or removed after the crystal formed. No lead was lost to weathering or metamorphism. For zircon, this is a defensible assumption: the crystal lattice excludes lead at high temperature, and the activation energy required to expel a uranium or lead atom from the lattice is far higher than the thermal energy present in most geological settings. Zircon is one of the most chemically inert minerals in the crust. It survives metamorphism, deformation, and millions of years of surface weathering. But "survives" is not "is sealed." Zircon can be damaged by radiation from its own uranium content, and damaged zircon can lose lead. That is why the concordia plot matters: a damaged grain will be discordant, and the population plot will tell you.

**2. The initial daughter product is known or zero.** When the crystal formed, it incorporated the parent (uranium) but not the daughter (lead), because the crystal lattice excludes lead. For zircon, this is well established. For other minerals, it is not — and the isochron method exists specifically to handle the case where the initial daughter is unknown. The isochron does not assume zero initial daughter. It measures it, from the spread of compositions across a suite of cogenetic rocks, and the intercept of the isochron gives the initial ratio directly, from the data. The assumption is replaced by a measurement.

The decay constant of uranium-238 is not measured on a rock. It is measured by counting decays. Take a known quantity of uranium-238 — a mass you weigh on a microbalance, a number of atoms you compute from Avogadro's constant — and count how many alpha particles it emits per second. The ratio is the decay constant. Avogadro's constant, in turn, is not determined from geology. It is determined by X-ray crystallography of silicon — by counting the number of atoms in a known volume of a silicon crystal — and by the Faraday constant, and by the charge of the electron, and by a dozen other routes that have converged, to the precision of the best measurements, on the same value.

The decay constant of uranium-235 is measured the same way: count the decays per atom per unit time. The half-life of 704 million years is not an assumption. It is a measured rate. The fact that the two uranium isotopes have different half-lives — 4.47 billion for U-238, 704 million for U-235 — is a measured fact, and it is the fact that makes the two-system concordia plot work.

The criticism that the decay constant is "calibrated against rocks of known age" is, in the standard case, a confusion of the measurement. The decay constant is a property of the nucleus. It is measured in the laboratory, in a counting experiment, with a known mass of material and a Geiger counter or an ionization chamber. The rock is not in the loop. The rock is the *application* of the measurement, not the *calibration* of it.

There is a more sophisticated version of the circularity objection, and it deserves a fair hearing. It goes like this: the decay constant is sometimes quoted from "the literature," and the literature value was originally determined by measurements that included rocks of known age. In that narrow sense, the literature value carries a small inherited dependency. But that dependency is traceable to a specific set of early measurements, and it has been superseded by direct counting experiments that do not involve rocks. The current accepted value is the one that comes from the counting experiments, not from the rock calibrations.

## Three cross-checks

**1. The concordance test.** A single isotope pair can be fooled by a single contamination event. Two independent isotope pairs cannot be fooled by the same event — unless the event is precisely tuned to preserve the ratio. The probability that a random contamination produces two concordant ages from two independent decay chains is vanishingly small. The fact that it happens, routinely, across zircon grains from a hundred different localities, is not a coincidence. It is a constraint.

**2. The zircon test.** Zircon (zirconium silicate) is the hardest mineral in the crust to contaminate. Its crystal lattice excludes lead at formation, is chemically inert to hydrothermal fluids, and survives metamorphism at temperatures that reset softer minerals. If a date is right for zircon, the date is right for the rock. The concordance of zircon ages from different localities, different geological contexts, and different analytical labs is not a coincidence. It is a constraint.

**3. The other clocks.** Potassium-argon, rubidium-strontium, samarium-neodymium, carbon-14, argon-argon, and a dozen other decay systems give ages that agree with the U-Pb dates on the same rock. Not perfectly — each has its own closed-system limits — but within the uncertainty of the measurement, they agree. A rock dated at 2.5 billion years by U-Pb zircon is dated at 2.5 billion years by Sm-Nd in the same rock. The agreement is not a prediction of the model. It is a constraint on the model.

## A reader's checklist

If you are evaluating any dating claim — in a paper, a popular article, a creationist debate, a geology exam — ask these questions in order:

1. **What was measured?** Ratios of isotopes, or absolute concentrations? Ratios are more robust.
2. **Which system?** U-Pb in zircon is the strongest. K-Ar in biotite is weaker. The mineral matters.
3. **Concordant or discordant?** A single concordant pair is strong. Multiple concordant pairs are much stronger.
4. **Closed system?** Has the rock been metamorphosed, hydrothermally altered, or weathered? If so, which minerals still preserve the original signature?
5. **Initial composition known?** Is the daughter isotope ratio measured by isochron, or assumed?
6. **Independent confirmation?** Do other dating systems on the same rock agree?

If the answer to all six is favorable, the age is robust. If not, the age is a lower bound, or an upper bound, or a range — and the paper should say which.

## Exercise

Take a published U-Pb zircon date you have read — from a paper, a museum label, a Wikipedia article. Now ask: what would have to be true for this date to be wrong? Not "what would a creationist say" — what, in the physical sense, would have to be the case? Write down the answer. You will find that the list is short, specific, and testable — and that none of the items on the list is "the decay constant is wrong by a factor of a hundred." The list is the argument.

## The verdict

The "4.5 billion years" figure is not a single number with a single provenance. It is the intersection of:

- A laboratory measurement (decay constants, Avogadro's constant, isotope ratios).
- A logical constraint (concordance, isochron, closed-system inference).
- A cross-check (multiple independent decay systems agreeing on the same rock).

The age is not *assumed*. It is not *calibrated against itself* in the circular sense. It is *measured*, in the same sense that the speed of light is measured — by counting, by weighing, by taking ratios, and by checking the result against an independent method. The error bars are not a sign of weakness. They are the signature of an honest measurement.

When a creationist says "they just assumed 4.5 billion years," the skeptic's answer is: "Show me the assumption. The decay constant is a counting experiment. The isotope ratio is a mass spectrometer reading. The concordance is arithmetic. The cross-check is three other decay systems agreeing. Where, in that chain, is the assumption?"

The answer is: nowhere. And that is why the age holds.

## Open questions

- **What is the decay constant of a radionuclide in a high-pressure environment?** Laboratory measurements are done at 1 atmosphere. The lower crust is at 3 GPa. The difference is expected to be negligible, but it is not zero. This is a real, open question — and it is small.
- **What is the initial lead ratio in a zircon from a specific locality?** The assumption of zero initial lead is well-established for zircon, but not universal. Isochron methods correct for it in other minerals. For zircon, it is a residual uncertainty, and it is small.
- **What is the closed-system behavior of a specific rock over 4 billion years?** Metamorphism, hydrothermal alteration, and weathering all reset some systems. The fact that zircon preserves the original signature is a measured fact, but it is a fact about a specific mineral in a specific context. The context matters.

These are not objections. They are the edges of the measurement — the places where the error bars widen, where the uncertainty grows, where the next experiment will go.
